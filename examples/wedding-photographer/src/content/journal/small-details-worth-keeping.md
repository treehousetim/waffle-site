Twenty years from now, you will not remember the colour of the napkins. You might, though, want to remember your grandmother's ring, the vows you wrote at the kitchen table, and the note your father slipped into your pocket. Details are a small part of any gallery, but a few of them become precious. Here is how we photograph them, and how you can help.

## The quiet hour

We arrive while you are still getting ready, and the first thing we do is the details: the rings, the invitations, the shoes, the flowers, the vows. Nobody is watching yet, the house is calm, and the light by a window is usually lovely. We spend half an hour on them, and then they are done, and we can give the rest of the day to people.

At Saoirse and Jun's wedding in Wrenmouth, their handwritten vows and the two rings sat on the windowsill of the loft while they dressed. That photograph is one of their favourites, and it took four minutes.

## Put it all in one box

The best thing you can do is gather everything into one box or bag before we arrive, and tell whoever is helping you where it is. We lose more time hunting for a ring box in someone's coat pocket than on any other part of the day. Into the box go:

- Both rings, and the engagement ring if you can bear to take it off for a few minutes.
- A full set of the stationery: invitation, order of service, menu, place card.
- The vows, if they are written down.
- Shoes, perfume, cufflinks, a watch, a tie.
- Anything borrowed, inherited or with a story: a grandmother's brooch, a father's handkerchief.

## The things with stories

Tell us about the things that matter. "This was my mum's" changes how we photograph it, and how we caption it for you later. One groom brought the pocket knife his grandfather carved the first table with; one bride brought her late father's watch and wore it on her wrist all day. We would never have known to look for either.

## The tables, before anyone sits

The room as it was meant to look exists for about ten minutes, between the moment the venue finishes laying it and the moment the guests come in. If you have spent months on the tables and the flowers, it is worth us slipping away from drinks to photograph the room then. At Ashcombe House the canopy of greenery and the candles were lit for supper, and we had the room to ourselves for five minutes. It is in the [story of that day](/stories/margot-and-dev/).

## What not to worry about

You do not need to buy props, or style anything for the camera. You do not need a special hanger for the dress (though it is nice to have a wooden one). Everything we photograph should be something that was really there, because that is what makes it worth keeping.

And if the day runs late and the details get five minutes instead of thirty: we will still find the ring, and the light, and the note in the pocket.
