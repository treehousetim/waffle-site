Every wedding timeline we write starts in the same place: the time the sun sets on your date, at your venue. Not the ceremony, not the supper, not the band. The sunset. Everything else gets placed around it, and the day almost always runs better for it.

That sounds like a photographer's vanity, and partly it is. But the light shapes how a day feels, not only how it looks. A ceremony at two in the afternoon in July is hot and squinting. The same ceremony at four is gentle, and your guests can see your faces.

## Start from sunset and work backwards

Look up the sunset time for your date (any weather site will tell you) and then work back from it like this:

- **Sunset minus 60 to 20 minutes:** the two of you, alone with us, for fifteen or twenty minutes. This is golden hour, when the light is low, warm and kind to everyone. It is where most of the photographs you will frame come from.
- **Sunset minus two hours or so:** supper starts, so that you can slip away between courses without anyone noticing.
- **Before that:** the family photographs, drinks and the ceremony.

On the Saltgrass Coast in midsummer the sun sets after eight, which gives you a long, easy afternoon. In October it is gone by half past six, and the whole day shifts earlier. We will do this arithmetic with you, but it helps to know why the times land where they do.

## A sample day, for a September wedding

Here is a real-shaped timeline for a wedding of about a hundred guests, with sunset at 7:25 p.m.:

- 12:30: we arrive. Details, and the last of getting ready.
- 2:00: the ceremony.
- 2:30: confetti, congratulations, drinks on the lawn.
- 3:15: family photographs, about twenty minutes.
- 3:45: ten minutes with the two of you, then back to your guests.
- 5:30: supper.
- 6:45: we steal you for golden hour, between the main and pudding.
- 7:30: speeches, while there is still a glow outside the windows.
- 8:30: cake and the first dance.
- 9:30: we leave you to it.

Notice there are two sessions with the two of you, not one long one. A short one straight after the ceremony catches the relief and the giddiness; the one at golden hour catches the light. Neither takes more than twenty minutes. Your guests will barely notice you have gone.

## Leave room for things to run late

Every wedding runs late, usually by about twenty minutes, and usually at the same points: getting ready, and the receiving line after the ceremony. We pad the timeline in those places so that the one thing that cannot move, the sun, does not have to.

If something does slip badly, we will tell you, quietly, what we would drop. Usually it is a set of group photographs that can happen later in the evening, or not at all.

## What to send us

When we plan your timeline together, it helps to have:

1. The ceremony time, and whether it can move.
2. Your venue's rules about the evening: last orders, when the band must stop.
3. Your list of family groups (there is a [whole note on those](/journal/family-photographs-without-the-chaos/)).
4. Anything you want photographed that is not obvious: a car, a dog, a grandmother's ring.

We will send back a timeline on one page, with the sunset marked on it, and a wet-weather version of the same day. Then we will all stop thinking about it, which is the point.
