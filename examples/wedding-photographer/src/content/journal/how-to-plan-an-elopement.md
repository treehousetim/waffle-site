An elopement is not a small wedding with the guests taken out. It is a different sort of day altogether: built around a place and a time rather than a guest list, and free to go at whatever pace the two of you like. Here is how we plan one, in roughly the order we would talk it through.

## Start with the feeling, then the place

Before you look at a map, it helps to say what you want the day to feel like. Wild and windswept? Quiet and green? A long walk, or a picnic and a bottle of something? Ines and Callum told us they wanted it to feel "like a long walk, not a production", and that sentence decided everything else.

Then we choose the place together. On this coast that might be a beach below the sea stacks, a headland above a cove, a ridge in the hills at sunrise, or the redwood groves inland, where it is always hushed. We know most of them well, including the ones that look wonderful on a postcard and are swarming with visitors at four on a Saturday.

## Then the time of day

The time matters as much as the place. A beach at low tide is a different beach. A ridge at sunrise is empty; the same ridge at noon has a car park full of hikers. We look at three things for your date:

- **The light:** sunrise and sunset times, and where the sun will be.
- **The tide:** on our beaches the best sand and the sea stacks only appear at low tide.
- **The crowds:** weekdays are quieter everywhere, and early mornings are quieter than evenings.

## The paperwork

Getting married legally usually needs a licence, an officiant and, in most places, witnesses. Rules vary, so check them for where you are marrying. Some couples sign the legal papers quietly at home the week before and keep the elopement itself for the vows. Both are fine.

Many parks and beaches need a permit for a ceremony or for professional photography, even with only two of you. We will tell you which ones do, and help you apply. It is rarely expensive, but it can take a few weeks.

## Witnesses, or not

If you need witnesses, we can be them, or you can bring two friends. A few couples bring their parents for the ceremony and then send everyone off to lunch while we go walking. There are no rules.

## Plan a backup spot

Weather on the coast changes fast. For every elopement we plan a second place, nearby and sheltered, and decide in the morning which to use. The backup is often a forest, which is beautiful in rain and fog.

## What to do with the rest of the day

This is the fun part. Book the long lunch. Hire a little cabin. Swim. Bring a cake and eat it on a rock. Some of our favourite elopement photographs are not of the vows at all but of the hours after, when the nerves have gone.

## The questions we ask every couple

1. What is the one photograph you would be sad not to have?
2. Are you happy to walk, and how far, in what shoes?
3. Who do you want to tell first, and how? (We have photographed a lot of video calls.)
4. Morning people or evening people?
5. Two days, or one?
