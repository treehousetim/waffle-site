An operating agreement is the contract between the owners of a limited liability company. It says who owns what, who decides what, how money comes in and goes out, and what happens when an owner wants to leave, dies, divorces or simply stops showing up. Most states do not require one. Almost every LLC needs one.

## What happens without one

An LLC with no operating agreement is not lawless. It is governed by the state's LLC statute, which supplies default rules for everything the owners did not decide for themselves. The trouble is that the defaults were written for no business in particular, and they rarely match what the owners assumed.

Depending on the state, the default rules may say that profits are shared equally regardless of who put in more money; that major decisions need every owner's consent, so one owner can block everything; or that an owner who leaves has no right to be bought out at all. Owners usually discover these rules at the worst possible moment: in the middle of a disagreement.

## The questions a good agreement answers

**Money in.** Who contributed what at the start, and what happens if the business needs more? Is anyone obliged to put in more, and if one owner does and another does not, does ownership change?

**Money out.** How are profits distributed, and when? Will the company distribute enough cash for owners to pay the tax on their share of profits, even in a year when the business would rather keep it?

**Decisions.** Which decisions can a manager make alone, which need a majority, and which need everyone? Selling the business, taking on debt and admitting a new owner usually belong in the last group.

**Work.** If some owners work in the business and others do not, how are the working owners paid? Salary, a guaranteed payment and a share of profits are different things, and confusion between them causes a great deal of resentment.

**Leaving.** This is the section that matters most and is most often missing. What happens when an owner wants out, retires, becomes disabled or dies? Who may buy the departing owner's share, at what price, and over what period? A clause that says the price will be "agreed between the parties" is a clause that guarantees a dispute.

## A word on templates

Online templates are better than nothing, and sometimes much better than nothing. Their weakness is that they answer the questions above in the way most convenient for a template: generically. The value of a lawyer here is not the drafting so much as the conversation. Many of the clients who come to us for an agreement change their minds about something important during the first meeting, because nobody had asked them the question before.

## When to write it

The best time is at formation, while everyone still agrees about everything. The second best time is now. We regularly write agreements for companies that have operated for a decade without one; it is never too late, though it is easier before there is anything to argue about.

## Keep it current

An operating agreement written for two founders may not fit the company once a third owner joins, a spouse inherits a share, or the business takes on a loan that comes with its own conditions. We suggest reading it again whenever ownership changes, and every few years in any case. It is a short read, if it was written well.
