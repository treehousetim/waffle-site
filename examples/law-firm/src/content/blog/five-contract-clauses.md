Most of the contracts a small business signs are not negotiated. They arrive from a supplier, a software company or a larger customer, and they are signed by someone with a dozen other things to do. We understand that; we sign the same kind of contracts ourselves. But if you only have time to read part of a contract carefully, read these five clauses. They are usually near the end, set in the same type as everything else, and they are where we see clients lose money.

## Limitation of liability

This clause caps what one side can recover from the other if something goes wrong, often at the fees paid in the previous few months, and often excludes "consequential" damages such as lost profits altogether. Read it from both directions. If the software that runs your scheduling fails for a week, what can you recover? Very possibly nothing beyond a refund. If you make a mistake on a customer's job, is your exposure capped, or unlimited?

A cap that applies to one side only is worth noticing, and worth asking about.

## Indemnification

An indemnity is a promise to pay for the other side's losses in certain situations, including, very often, their legal fees. Broad indemnities ("any and all claims arising out of or relating to this agreement") can make you responsible for problems you did not cause. Check whether the indemnity is limited to your own negligence or breach, whether it is mutual, and whether your insurance would cover it. Many general liability policies do not cover contractual indemnities without an endorsement.

## Automatic renewal

Many service contracts renew automatically for another full term unless you give notice, in writing, within a short window before the term ends. Miss the window by a day and you may be committed to another year or three. When you sign a contract like this, put the notice date in your calendar, not the end date. Better still, ask for renewal to be month to month.

## Termination

Read what it takes to end the contract early, and what it costs. Can either side terminate on notice, or only for a breach? Is there a cure period, a chance to fix a problem before the other side can walk away? What do you owe if you terminate early: the remaining fees for the whole term? A contract that is easy to sign and expensive to leave deserves a second look before the signature, not after.

## Governing law and disputes

This clause decides which state's law applies and where any dispute will be heard: a court several states away, or an arbitration under rules that require large filing fees. For a small business, being required to bring every dispute in another state can make even a strong claim not worth pursuing. Sometimes this clause is negotiable, particularly if you ask for disputes to be heard where the work is done.

## Asking is allowed

Many people assume a printed contract is take it or leave it. Often it is not, especially with smaller suppliers and customers. A short, polite email asking for a mutual limitation of liability, or for renewal on thirty days' notice, succeeds more often than you might expect. And if the answer is no, you will at least know what you are signing.

When a contract is large, long or unusual, we review it for a flat fee agreed in advance, and return it with a short memo saying what matters and what does not. Most of what we write is: this is fine.
