Small employers often ask whether they need a handbook at all. Twelve people, everyone knows everyone, why write it down? The answer is that a handbook is one of the cheapest protections an employer can have, provided it is short, accurate and followed. The handbooks that cause trouble are the long ones, copied from a much larger company, full of promises nobody remembers making.

## What a handbook is for

A handbook does three jobs. It tells employees what is expected of them and what they can expect in return. It tells managers how to handle common situations consistently. And, when a dispute arises, it shows that the rules were written down, communicated and applied evenly. That last job is the one that matters most when an employee files a complaint, and it is the one a handbook only does if it matches what actually happens in the business.

## What to include

**Employment at will.** In most states, employment can be ended by either side at any time, for any lawful reason. Say so clearly, and avoid language elsewhere that undermines it, such as promises of discipline in steps or "permanent" status after a probationary period.

**Equal opportunity and anti-harassment.** A clear policy, a way to complain that does not require going through the person complained about, and a promise of no retaliation. These policies matter in court, and they matter more in the workplace.

**Pay and hours.** When people are paid, how time is recorded, how overtime is approved, and the rule that all time worked must be recorded and will be paid, approved or not.

**Leave and time off.** What you offer, and what the law requires of an employer your size. Requirements differ by state and by headcount; this section is where copied handbooks most often go wrong.

**Conduct, confidentiality and equipment.** Kept short. A few sentences on the use of company phones, vehicles and accounts saves arguments later.

**An acknowledgement.** A page each employee signs to say they received the handbook. Keep it in their file.

## What to leave out

Leave out policies you will not follow. A detailed progressive discipline policy is a promise; if you then dismiss someone without the warnings it describes, the handbook becomes evidence against you. Leave out policies you do not need: a twelve-person shop does not need the travel policy of a multinational. And leave out anything you have not checked against your own state's law.

## Keep it current

Employment law changes every year, at the federal level and in the states. A handbook written five years ago may now be wrong about leave, pay transparency or something else entirely. We suggest a review every year or two, and whenever the business crosses a headcount threshold.

## Train the managers

The best handbook in the world does nothing if the managers have not read it. An hour with your supervisors on what it says, particularly about complaints and time records, is worth more than another ten pages.

Our handbooks for small employers are usually under twenty pages, written in plain English and quoted at a flat fee. Employees read them. That is rather the point.
