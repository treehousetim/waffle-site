Nearly every new business owner who sits down with us has already been told, by a friend, a website or a brother-in-law, that they need an LLC. Often they do. But the choice of entity is a decision with consequences for taxes, for raising money, for bringing in partners and for selling the business one day, and the right answer depends on a handful of questions that are worth asking out loud.

## What the entity actually does

A limited liability company and a corporation do the same basic job: they are a separate legal person that owns the business, signs its contracts and owes its debts. Done properly, that separation means a customer who sues the business, or a lender it cannot repay, generally cannot reach the owners' houses and savings.

The protection is not automatic. Owners who mix personal and business money, sign contracts in their own names or let the paperwork lapse can lose it. The entity is a shield only if it is treated like one.

## Question one: how will you be taxed?

This is the question that matters most, and it is one for your accountant as much as for us. An LLC can be taxed in several ways: as a sole proprietorship or partnership by default, or, by election, as an S corporation or a C corporation. A corporation is taxed as a C corporation unless it elects S status.

Those choices change how profits are taxed, whether owners pay self-employment tax on all of their income, and what happens to money the business keeps rather than distributes. We do not recommend an entity until your accountant has run the numbers, and we are happy to be in the same meeting.

## Question two: who owns it, and who will?

One owner, two founders who split everything evenly, a family with three generations involved, an investor who wants a return but no say: each of these points in a different direction. LLCs are flexible about how ownership, profits and votes are divided. Corporations are more rigid, which can be a virtue when there are many owners and a drawback when there are few.

## Question three: will you raise outside money?

Professional investors usually expect a corporation, often one formed in a particular state, with classes of stock they understand. If you expect to raise venture money, that expectation may decide the matter. Most of our clients will never do so, and for them it should not.

## Question four: how do you expect it to end?

Some businesses are built to be sold; some to be handed to a daughter; some to be wound down when the founder retires. The entity and its governing agreement should say what happens in each case. A sale of an LLC and a sale of a corporation are taxed differently, and the difference can be large.

## Question five: how much formality will you keep?

Corporations come with board meetings, minutes and annual filings. LLCs ask less, though they still require an annual report and a registered agent. An entity whose formalities nobody keeps is an entity whose protection is at risk. We would rather recommend the structure you will actually maintain.

## What we usually find

For a single owner or a small group of people who know each other, a limited liability company with a well-written operating agreement is very often the answer, and S corporation tax treatment is often worth discussing once profits are steady. But "very often" is not "always". The fifteen minutes spent on these five questions are the cheapest part of starting a business, and the part that is most expensive to get wrong.

> The label on the filing is the least important decision. The agreement between the owners is the most.
