It is the first question in most of our estate planning meetings, and families have usually heard strong opinions on both sides. The honest answer is that a will and a revocable trust are two good tools that do overlapping jobs, and the right choice depends on the family, the property and the people involved.

## What a will does

A will says who receives your property when you die, who will be in charge of distributing it (your executor) and, if you have young children, who you would like to raise them. It takes effect only at death, and it works through the probate court: your executor presents the will to the court, is formally appointed, gathers your property, pays your debts and distributes what remains.

A will is relatively simple and inexpensive to prepare. For many families it is all they need.

## What a revocable trust does

A revocable living trust is an arrangement you create during your life. You transfer your property into the trust, usually naming yourself as trustee, so in daily life nothing changes: you still buy, sell and spend as before. You also name a successor trustee who takes over if you die or become unable to manage your affairs.

Because the trust, not you, owns the property when you die, that property generally passes to your beneficiaries without going through probate. You can change or revoke the trust at any time while you are able to.

## The differences that matter

**Probate.** The main reason families choose a trust is to avoid probate. How much that is worth depends on the state. In some states probate is slow and expensive; in others it is a manageable process for an ordinary estate. Owning real estate in more than one state often tips the balance toward a trust, because otherwise your family may face probate in each state.

**Privacy.** A will becomes a public court record. A trust generally does not. For most families this matters less than they expect, but for some it matters a great deal.

**Incapacity.** A trust names someone to manage the property in it if you can no longer do so. A will does nothing until death. Families without a trust rely instead on a durable power of attorney for this, which works well if it is drafted and kept properly.

**Cost and effort.** A trust costs more to prepare, and it only works if you actually transfer your property into it. We see too many trusts that were signed and never funded: a binder on a shelf, with the house still in the parents' names. An unfunded trust avoids nothing.

## Most families need a will either way

Even families with a trust need a short will, often called a pour-over will, to catch anything left outside the trust and, importantly, to name guardians for minor children. And every family, whichever route it takes, needs the documents that work during life: a durable power of attorney and a health care directive.

## How we help you decide

We start with what you own, where it is and who you want to receive it, and then the people: a child who would make an excellent trustee, a child who would not, a second marriage, a grandchild with special needs. By the end of the first meeting most families know which route suits them. If a will is enough, we will say so. A plan you understand and keep up to date is worth more than an elaborate one you do not.
