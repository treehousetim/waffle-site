For many small businesses, the lease is the largest financial commitment they will ever make: five or ten years of rent, personally guaranteed, for a space they have looked at twice. And commercial leases offer very little of the protection a residential tenant takes for granted. The law assumes two businesses can look after themselves, so the lease itself is very nearly the whole of the deal. Here is where to look.

## The rent is not only the rent

Many commercial leases are "net" leases: on top of the base rent, the tenant pays a share of the landlord's operating costs, usually property taxes, insurance and common area maintenance. These charges can be a third or more of what you actually pay, and they can rise every year.

Ask for the last two or three years of actual operating expenses, not an estimate. Ask what is included, and look for exclusions: a tenant should not usually pay for the landlord's loan, capital improvements to the building, or the cost of leasing other space. A cap on increases in controllable expenses is worth requesting.

## Who repairs what

Read the repair and maintenance section closely, especially for the roof, the structure and the heating and cooling system. In some leases the tenant is responsible for replacing an air-conditioning unit that fails in the third year, at a cost of many months' rent. If the lease puts major systems on you, ask for an inspection before signing, and a warranty or a cap.

## Use and exclusivity

The "permitted use" clause says what you may do in the space. Write it broadly enough for the business you hope to become, not only the one you are today. If you depend on being the only business of your kind in the center, ask for an exclusive use clause preventing the landlord from leasing to a competitor.

## Assignment and subletting

If you sell your business, the buyer will usually need to take over the lease. Many leases forbid assignment without the landlord's consent, and some let the landlord refuse for any reason or take a share of the profit. Ask for consent "not to be unreasonably withheld", and for a sale of the business to be a permitted transfer.

## The personal guarantee

Landlords often require the owners of a small business to guarantee the lease personally. If the business fails, the landlord can pursue the owners for the rent for the rest of the term. Guarantees are often negotiable: limited to a fixed amount, to the first years of the lease, or ending after a period of on-time payment.

## Renewal, and the end

An option to renew lets you stay at a known or calculable rent; without one, the landlord can set any rent, or let the space to someone else, when the term ends. Check how and when you must exercise the option, usually by written notice months in advance, and put that date in your calendar. Then read what you must do when you leave: restore the space, remove fixtures, repair wear.

## Get the letter of intent right

Most of these points are easiest to negotiate before the lease is drafted, in the letter of intent. Once a landlord's standard lease arrives, every change is a request. We review leases for tenants for a flat fee, and we are happiest when we see the letter of intent first.
