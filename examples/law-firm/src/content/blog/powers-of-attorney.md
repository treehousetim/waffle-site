When people think about estate planning, they think about wills: what happens after death. Yet in our experience the documents families most often need, and most often do not have, are the ones that work while you are still alive. A stroke, a fall, dementia, or a long illness can leave a person unable to sign a check or agree to a treatment. Without the right documents, the people who love them may have no legal authority to help.

## The durable power of attorney

A power of attorney is a document in which you, the principal, give someone you trust, your agent, the authority to act for you in financial and legal matters: paying bills, managing bank accounts, dealing with the tax authorities, selling a car, signing a deed.

"Durable" means it continues to work if you become incapacitated, which is precisely when it is needed. A power of attorney that is not durable ends when you lose capacity, and so fails at the moment it matters most.

Some powers of attorney take effect immediately; others "spring" into effect only when a doctor certifies that you are incapacitated. Springing powers sound attractive, but they can cause delays at the worst moment while banks wait for the right letter. Many families choose an immediate power, held by the lawyer or kept in a drawer until it is needed.

## The health care directive

A health care directive, called an advance directive or health care power of attorney in some states, does two things. It names someone to make medical decisions for you if you cannot, and it records your wishes about treatment, particularly at the end of life: resuscitation, ventilation, artificial nutrition.

The second part is often called a living will. It spares your family from guessing, and from arguing, about what you would have wanted.

## What happens without them

Without a power of attorney, a family may have to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator. That process is public, can take weeks or months, costs money, and ends with a court, not you, choosing who manages your affairs and supervising them closely. It is sometimes necessary. It is almost always avoidable.

Joint bank accounts are sometimes suggested as a shortcut. They can work for paying bills, but they give the other owner full rights to the money, can expose it to that person's creditors, and may pass the account to them at your death regardless of your will.

## Choosing your agents

Choose people who are trustworthy, organized and willing, in that order. They do not have to be the same person for money and for medicine; a level-headed daughter may be the right health care agent, while a son who is an accountant manages finances. Name a backup for each. And talk to them: an agent who knows your wishes is far more useful than one who discovers the document in a crisis.

## Keep them where they can be found

Give copies of your health care directive to your doctor and your agent. Tell your financial agent where the original power of attorney is. Review both every few years, and after a marriage, divorce or death in the family.

These documents are short, inexpensive, and part of every estate plan we prepare. If you have a will but not these, they are the gap most worth closing.
