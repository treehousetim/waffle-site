Buying a commercial building, a warehouse or a parcel of land for a business is not like buying a home. There is no standard form contract written to protect the buyer, few disclosures required of the seller, and a short window, set by the purchase contract, in which to discover everything you need to know. That window is called the due diligence period, and what you do with it decides whether you know what you are buying.

## The contract comes first

The single most useful thing a buyer can do is talk to a lawyer before signing the purchase contract. The contract decides how long the due diligence period lasts, what the seller must hand over, whether your deposit is refundable if you walk away, and what happens if a problem turns up. A thirty-day period that seemed generous at signing can disappear quickly while waiting for a survey.

## Title

A title company searches the public records and issues a title commitment: a promise to insure your ownership, subject to a list of exceptions. Those exceptions are the important part. They may include easements that let others cross the land, restrictive covenants that limit its use, old mortgages that were never released, or a neighbor's right of way. Each one should be read, understood and either accepted or resolved before closing.

## Survey

A current survey shows where the boundaries actually are, where the buildings sit, and whether anything crosses a line: a fence, a driveway, a neighbor's shed, or your own building. It also shows whether the property lies in a flood zone. Ask the surveyor to plot every easement listed in the title commitment, so you can see what each one means on the ground.

## Zoning and permits

Check that your intended use is permitted under the zoning rules, not just the current one. A building used as a warehouse may not be zoned for the retail showroom you have in mind. Ask the county whether there are open code violations or unfinished permits, and whether parking, signs and access meet current requirements. A zoning letter from the county is often worth requesting.

## Environmental history

For many commercial properties, a lender will require a Phase I environmental site assessment, a review of the property's past uses for signs of contamination. Even if your lender does not, consider one. A former gas station, dry cleaner or machine shop can carry cleanup costs far larger than the purchase price, and an owner can be responsible for contamination it did not cause.

## Leases and tenants

If the property has tenants, read every lease and ask each tenant to sign an estoppel certificate confirming the rent, the term and that the landlord is not in default. You are buying their leases along with the building, including any promises the seller made to them.

## Keep the calendar

The most common problem we see is not a hidden defect but a missed deadline: an objection to title raised a day too late, a financing contingency that lapsed. On the first day of every purchase we send our clients a calendar of every date in the contract. We keep it, and we make sure they do too.

A careful due diligence period costs a small fraction of the purchase price. It is the part of the transaction that protects all the rest.
