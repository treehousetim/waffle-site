When someone dies, the person named as executor in their will is often a grieving son, daughter or spouse who has never done anything like it before. The word "probate" sounds formidable. In practice it is a sequence of fairly ordinary tasks, carried out under the supervision of a court. Here is roughly what happens, in roughly the order it happens. The details vary by state; the shape does not.

## Find the original will

The court needs the original, signed will, not a copy. Look in the deceased's papers, a safe deposit box, or with the lawyer who drafted it; many firms, ours included, keep clients' original wills in a fireproof vault. If there is no will, the estate still goes through probate, but state law decides who inherits and who may serve as administrator.

## Secure the property, and wait

Lock the house, keep it insured, and make sure nobody removes anything, however well meant. Do not pay the deceased's debts or distribute anything yet, even if relatives ask. An executor who pays the wrong creditor first, or hands over an heirloom too soon, can be personally responsible for the consequences.

## Open the estate

The executor files the will with the probate court in the county where the deceased lived, and asks to be appointed. Once appointed, the executor receives a document, often called letters testamentary, that proves their authority to banks and others. In some states the executor must post a bond; many wills waive that requirement.

## Notify the people who must be told

Beneficiaries named in the will, and close relatives who would inherit if there were no will, are usually entitled to notice. Creditors must be given the chance to make claims, often by a notice published in a local newspaper and by letters to known creditors. The time allowed for claims sets the pace of much of what follows.

## Gather and value the property

The executor opens an estate bank account, collects the deceased's money into it, and makes an inventory of everything the deceased owned, with its value at the date of death. Some property does not pass through probate at all: accounts with a named beneficiary, property held jointly with a right of survivorship, and anything in a trust. Those pass directly, though they may still matter for tax.

## Pay debts, expenses and taxes

Once the claims period has run, the executor pays valid debts, the costs of administering the estate and any taxes due, including the deceased's final income tax return and, for larger estates, estate tax returns. If there is not enough money to pay everyone, state law sets the order of priority.

## Distribute what remains

Only now does the executor distribute property to the beneficiaries, as the will directs. Beneficiaries usually sign a receipt for what they are given. Real estate is transferred by a deed from the executor.

## Account and close

Finally, the executor reports to the court, and often to the beneficiaries, what came in, what went out and where it went. When the court accepts the accounting, the executor is discharged and the estate is closed.

## How long it takes

For a straightforward estate, often between six months and a year, much of it spent waiting for the creditors' period to end. Disputes, a business, property in several states or a tax return can make it longer.

## You do not have to do it alone

An executor may hire a lawyer, and the estate, not the executor personally, ordinarily pays reasonable legal fees.
