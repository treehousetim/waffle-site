Few questions cost small employers more, more often, than this one. A business pays someone by the job, without withholding taxes or offering benefits, and calls them an independent contractor. Years later a tax audit, an unemployment claim or an injury forces the question: was that person really an employee? If the answer is yes, the business may owe back taxes, penalties, overtime and more.

## The label is not the answer

The first thing to understand is that what you call the relationship, and even what the written agreement says, does not decide it. Government agencies and courts look at how the work is actually done. A contract that says "independent contractor" is evidence, but it does not settle the matter if everything else points the other way.

## How the question is decided

There is no single test. Different laws use different tests, and the federal tax authorities, the federal wage laws and the state can each reach their own conclusion about the same worker. But they all circle around the same idea: is this person in business for themselves, or are they working in your business?

The factors that tend to point toward **employee**:

- You control how, when and where the work is done, not just the result.
- The work is a core part of what your business does.
- The person works only or mainly for you, on an ongoing basis.
- You provide the tools, the equipment and the training.
- They are paid by the hour or the week, with no chance of profit or loss.

The factors that tend to point toward **contractor**:

- They decide how to do the work and supply their own tools.
- They have other clients, advertise their services and can turn work down.
- They are paid by the project and can make or lose money on it.
- The work is specialized and outside your usual operations.
- They have their own business entity, insurance and licenses.

No one factor decides it. A bookkeeper who comes in two afternoons a week to use your computer, at hours you set, looks like a part-time employee. The same bookkeeper working from her own office for twenty clients looks like a contractor.

## Why it matters

Employers must withhold income tax and pay their share of payroll taxes, pay minimum wage and overtime to non-exempt employees, carry workers' compensation insurance and pay unemployment insurance. None of those obligations applies to a genuine contractor. When a worker is reclassified, the business can owe all of it, backdated, with interest and penalties. Some states treat misclassification as a separate violation in its own right.

## What to do now

If you use contractors, review each relationship against the factors above, honestly. Where the answer is unclear, the safer course is often to treat the person as an employee, or to change how the work is arranged. Keep written agreements that describe the relationship accurately, and make sure practice matches paper.

A short review now is far cheaper than an audit later. When an employer brings us their list of contractors, we usually find that most are fine, one or two are not, and the fix is simpler than they feared.
