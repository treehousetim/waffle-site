When we first walked into the old Wrenford Iron & Stove Works in 2018, the roof leaked, there were trees growing through the floor of the pattern shop, and pigeons owned the gantry. It had been empty since 1967. We fell for it immediately.

This is a short history of the building, put together from the town library, the county historical society and a lot of conversations with people at the bar whose grandparents worked here.

## Built by the river, for the river

The stove works opened in 1888 at the bottom of what was then called Ferry Lane. The site was chosen for the Tamsen: barges could unload pig iron, coke and sand at the dock, and loaded stoves could go back out the same way. The loading dock where you can now drink a pint in the sun was built for exactly that.

The building is a long brick hall with steel roof trusses and tall windows down both sides, to let out heat and let in light for the moulders. At the north end stood the cupola, a tall cylindrical furnace where iron was melted with coke and limestone. The black soot on the brick above where it stood has never washed off. We have stopped trying.

## How a stove was made

Every stove started as a wooden pattern, carved in the pattern shop: the room we now hire out for private events, which still has the carpenters' benches along one wall. The patterns were pressed into damp sand in iron boxes to make moulds. Molten iron from the cupola was carried across the floor in ladles, or run along a channel cut into the floor, and poured into the moulds. That channel is the pour line, and it still runs past our bar, under a coat of epoxy.

At its peak in the early 1930s the works ran two shifts, employed around two hundred people and shipped a finished parlour stove roughly every hour.

## The end, and the long wait

Stove-making declined steadily as homes moved to gas and oil heat after the Second World War. The cupola was blown out for the last time in December 1967, and the building was sold, then sold again, and mostly left alone. It survived because it was too solid to be worth knocking down.

## What we kept

When we brought the brewery in, we kept everything we could:

- **The steel trusses and the gantry crane**, which now carries hoses instead of ladles.
- **The pour line** in the floor, which gave our pilsner its name.
- **The pattern shop's timber**, planed down and rebuilt as the taproom tables.
- **The ferry rope** that we found coiled in the cellar, now hung above the bar.
- **A single cast-iron stove**, a 1926 parlour model with the works' name on the door, which a customer gave us and which heats the pattern shop every winter.

If you have a story about the works, or a photograph, or a stove, we would love to hear about it. Ask for June at the bar.
