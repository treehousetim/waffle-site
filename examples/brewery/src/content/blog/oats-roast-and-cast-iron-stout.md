Hold a pint of Cast Iron Stout up to the light and you will not see through it. People often expect it to be heavy, bitter or strong because it is black. It is none of those things. At 6.2% it is only a little stronger than our IPA, and most of its character comes from careful choices about grain.

## Colour comes from the kiln

All beer starts with malted barley: grain that has been soaked, allowed to sprout, then dried in a kiln. Dry it gently and you get pale malt, which gives sugar for fermentation and a light, bready flavour. Keep going, hotter and longer, and the malt toasts, then caramelises, then roasts, exactly like bread turning to toast and then to charcoal.

Cast Iron uses seven malts:

- **Maris Otter**, a rich English pale malt, for the base: most of the sugar and a biscuity backbone.
- **Brown malt**, for a dry, nutty toast.
- **Crystal 60**, for a touch of caramel sweetness and body.
- **Chocolate malt**, roasted until it tastes of cocoa.
- **Roasted barley**, unmalted and roasted, for the coffee note and the dry finish.
- **Black malt**, a pinch, for colour and an espresso edge.
- **Flaked oats**, about one part in eight.

The dark malts are only about a tenth of the grist. A little goes a long way; more would make the beer taste burnt and ashy.

## Why oats

Oats are rich in beta-glucans and proteins, which survive into the finished beer and give it a thicker, silkier feel on the tongue. They do not add much flavour of their own, but they change everything about how the roast is perceived: the same dark malts in a beer without oats taste sharper and thinner.

The catch is that oats are sticky. They turn a mash into porridge and slow the flow of wort out of the mash tun to a trickle. We add rice hulls, which do nothing for flavour but keep the grain bed loose enough to drain. Brew day for Cast Iron is the longest of any core beer apart from the pilsner.

## The nitrogen pour

Most beer is carbonated with carbon dioxide alone. Cast Iron is served from a keg pushed with a mix of about 70% nitrogen and 30% carbon dioxide, through a tap with a restrictor plate that forces the beer through tiny holes.

Nitrogen does not dissolve well in beer, so the bubbles that form are very small. That is what makes the cascade you see when it is first poured, the tight tan head, and the creamy texture. It also makes the beer taste a little less bitter and a little less acidic, because carbon dioxide forms a mild acid in beer and there is less of it.

## How to drink it

Let it settle. Watch the cascade, wait until the beer below the head is black all the way up, then drink it in big mouthfuls rather than sips so you get the head and the beer together. And let it warm up a little as you go: at 50°F the chocolate and coffee are at their best.

In the winter it comes in cans, but cans cannot do the nitro pour. For the real thing, you will have to come to the bar.
