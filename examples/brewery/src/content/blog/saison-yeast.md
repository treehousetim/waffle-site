Brewers like to say that they make wort and yeast makes beer. Nowhere is that more true than with a saison, where the yeast provides most of the flavour, and does it on its own terms.

Bellows Saison was the first beer we ever sold, out of a garage in 2016. It has taught us more about yeast than every other beer put together.

## A little history

Saisons were the beers of farms in the French-speaking south of Belgium, brewed in the cooler months and stored for the farmhands who worked the summer harvest. Every farm had its own yeast and its own recipe, and the beers were dry, refreshing and fairly low in alcohol. Today's saisons are usually stronger and drier, but they share the same idea: a beer that quenches a thirst.

## What the yeast does

Saison yeast belongs to a family that produces a lot of flavour compounds called phenols and esters as it ferments. In Bellows that means:

- **Black pepper and clove**, from phenols.
- **Lemon zest and pear**, from esters.
- **A very dry finish**, because saison yeast is unusually good at eating complex sugars that other ale yeasts leave behind. It is not unusual for a saison to ferment almost every bit of sugar in the wort.

That dryness is why Bellows tastes lighter than its 6.1% strength. There is almost no sweetness left to weigh it down.

## And why it is a diva

The traditional saison strains are famous among brewers for one particular habit: they ferment furiously for a few days and then stop, a little short of finished, and sit there. A brewer new to them can wait for weeks for the last few points of gravity to drop.

What they want is heat. Where most ale yeasts would turn hot and solventy at high temperatures, saison yeast thrives on it. We start Bellows at 68°F and let it rise, day by day, to as high as 90°F. In the summer we barely have to help. In January, we wrap the fermenter in a heating jacket and treat it like a houseplant.

We also do not rush it off the yeast. Bellows spends about three weeks in the fermenter before it is chilled, and we check the gravity until it has been steady for three days running.

## The rest of the recipe

The rest of the beer stays out of the way: pilsner malt, a little wheat and spelt for body and a slightly grainy, rustic character, a touch of Munich malt for colour, and noble hops (Saaz and Styrian Goldings) for a gentle, herbal bitterness. Our water gets a small addition of gypsum to sharpen the finish.

## How to drink it

In a tulip glass, at 45 to 48°F, poured with a big, rocky head. Saison loves food more than almost any other beer: mussels and fries, roast pork with herbs, a soft goat cheese, or the Saturday truck's wood-fired pizza. It is also our most popular bottle to take home for a dinner party, because it goes with almost everything you are likely to cook.
