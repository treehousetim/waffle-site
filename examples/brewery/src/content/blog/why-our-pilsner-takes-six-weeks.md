People are sometimes surprised to learn that the cheapest beer on our board takes the longest to make. Pour Line Pilsner costs the same as a pint of Cupola IPA, but it occupies a fermenter for three times as long. Every week a tank holds pilsner is a week it cannot hold anything else, so it is a fair question: why bother?

The short answer is that a pilsner has nowhere to hide. There is no wall of hops, no roast, no fruit. It is pale malt, a little Saaz, soft water and a clean lager yeast, and every flaw shows. The long answer is below.

## Brew day: the decoction

Most of our beers are mashed in one step: crushed malt and hot water held at one temperature for an hour. Pour Line gets a decoction. Partway through the mash, we pull about a third of the thick grain out into the kettle, bring it to a boil, and pour it back. It raises the temperature of the whole mash in steps, and boiling the grain brings out the deeper, bready, almost honeyed flavour that marks a Bohemian pilsner.

It adds two hours to a brew day and a lot of stirring. We have tried it without. It tastes thinner, and our regulars noticed before we told them.

## Fermentation: cold and slow

Ale yeast likes the high sixties Fahrenheit and works fast, throwing off fruity compounds as it goes. Lager yeast works at around 50°F, and it works slowly. Two weeks of primary fermentation is normal for us, compared with five or six days for an ale.

At the end of fermentation we let the tank warm a few degrees for a couple of days. This is called a diacetyl rest. Diacetyl is a compound that tastes of butter or butterscotch, and lager yeast will clean it up if it is given the warmth and time to do it. Skip the rest and the beer can taste like movie-theatre popcorn. Nobody wants that in a pilsner.

## Lagering: the part you cannot fake

Then the beer is chilled to just above freezing and left alone for about four weeks. *Lager* comes from the German for *to store*, and this is the storing.

During those weeks, yeast and proteins drop out of the beer, sulfur notes from fermentation fade, the carbonation knits in finely, and the hop bitterness rounds off. You can taste the difference week by week if you pull samples, which we do, every Monday. By week three it is good. By week five it is the beer we want to put our name on.

## Why we will not shorten it

There are shortcuts. Warmer fermentation, finings to drop the haze, forced carbonation, a filter. Some of them are perfectly respectable. But each takes a little of the softness out of the beer, and softness is what makes a pilsner worth drinking a second and third of.

So Pour Line sits in its tank for six weeks, and we plan the rest of the schedule around it. When you see it on the board, that is what you are drinking: six weeks of patience, in a tall glass, at 42°F.

If you want to taste the difference for yourself, ask for a taster of Pour Line next to whatever guest lager is on. Then tell us which one you would rather have a pint of.
