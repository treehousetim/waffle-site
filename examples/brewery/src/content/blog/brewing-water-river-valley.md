When people tour the brewery they want to see the hops and the tanks. Nobody asks to see the water. But water is more than ninety percent of every beer we make, and the minerals dissolved in it shape how a beer tastes almost as much as the malt and hops do.

Historically, water decided what a town brewed. The famous styles of Europe grew up around their wells: the very soft water of Plzeň made pale, delicate lagers possible; the hard, sulfate-rich water of Burton upon Trent made sharply bitter pale ales; the carbonate-heavy water of Dublin and London suited dark, roasty beers. Brewers worked with what came out of the ground.

## What comes out of our tap

Wrenford's water is drawn from wells in the sand and gravel beside the Tamsen. By the time it reaches Foundry Row it is moderately soft, low in sulfate, with a little calcium and a gentle alkalinity. We send a sample to a lab every spring and every autumn, because it shifts with the seasons and the river level.

For a brewery, that is a good place to start. Soft water is easy to build up and very hard to take minerals out of.

## The first thing we do

Everything goes through a carbon filter. City water is treated with chloramine to keep it safe on its way through the pipes, which is exactly what you want from a public water supply, and exactly what you do not want in a mash: chloramine reacts with compounds in malt to make chlorophenols, which taste of plasters and swimming pools at levels most people can detect. Carbon removes it.

## Then we build it, beer by beer

Each recipe has its own water profile, adjusted with small, carefully weighed additions of brewing salts:

- **Pour Line Pilsner** gets almost nothing. Our water is close to what a Bohemian pilsner wants, so we use it nearly as it comes, with a touch of acid to bring the mash pH down.
- **Cupola IPA** gets gypsum (calcium sulfate). Sulfate makes hop bitterness taste firmer and drier, and is the secret behind the snap of a West Coast IPA.
- **Tamsen Haze** gets calcium chloride instead. Chloride rounds and softens the body, which is what makes a hazy beer feel juicy rather than sharp.
- **Cast Iron Stout** needs a little more alkalinity, because dark roasted malts are acidic and would otherwise drag the mash pH too low and make the beer taste harsh.

The amounts are tiny, measured in grams per barrel, but you would taste the difference if we got them wrong.

## Why it matters to you

You will never order a beer by its sulfate-to-chloride ratio. But if you have ever wondered why our pilsner tastes soft and our IPA tastes crisp, part of the answer is in a lab report pinned above the brewhouse sink, and in the river running past the window.
