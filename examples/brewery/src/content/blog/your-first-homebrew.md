Every brewer at Ironwood started at home. Hal brewed in the garage on Mill Street; I started with a stockpot on a student apartment stove that set off the smoke alarm every time. A lot of people at the bar tell us they would like to try it, and ask where to start. Here is what we tell them.

## Start with extract, or a small all-grain batch

There are two main ways to brew at home. **Extract brewing** uses malt extract, a syrup or powder made from wort, so you skip the mash and go straight to boiling with hops. **All-grain brewing** starts from crushed malt, as we do, and gives you more control, but needs more kit and more time.

Either is fine for a first batch. If you want to start all-grain, try a one-gallon batch first. You can do it with a large pot, a fine mesh bag and a few kitchen tools, and if it goes wrong you have lost an afternoon rather than a weekend.

## Cleaning is ninety percent of brewing

Once your wort has cooled below about 140°F, anything it touches must be sanitised: fermenter, lid, airlock, spoon, thermometer, siphon and your hands. Wild yeast and bacteria love cooling wort as much as your yeast does, and they make beer taste sour, medicinal or like nail varnish.

Buy a no-rinse sanitiser from a homebrew shop and use it on everything. It is the cheapest insurance in brewing. If you are not sure whether to sanitise something, do.

## Control the temperature

The single biggest improvement most homebrewers can make is controlling fermentation temperature. Yeast produces heat as it works, and a fermenter in a warm kitchen can run ten degrees hotter than the room. Too warm and you get harsh, fruity, solventy flavours.

For a first ale, aim for the low to mid sixties Fahrenheit. A cool basement is ideal. A tub of water with a frozen bottle swapped in each morning works surprisingly well.

## Be patient

Fermentation bubbling stops long before the beer is done. Leave it in the fermenter for at least two weeks, then bottle it with a little priming sugar and leave the bottles at room temperature for another two weeks to carbonate. Then chill them for a few days.

Almost every homebrewer drinks their first batch too early. It always tastes better at six weeks than at four.

## Take notes

Write down everything: what you bought, how much, the temperatures, the times, the gravity readings, what went wrong. Your notebook is how a lucky batch becomes a recipe you can repeat.

## The one piece of kit worth buying

A **hydrometer**. It measures how much sugar is in the wort, which tells you how strong the beer will be, whether fermentation has finished and whether anything has gone wrong. It costs about the same as a six-pack.
