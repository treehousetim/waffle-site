Look at a dark car in low afternoon sun and you will often see a web of fine circular scratches around the reflection of the light. Detailers call them swirl marks. Almost all of them are put there in the same place: during washing.

Grit is the problem. Road dust, brake dust and the fine sand that blows across every parking lot sit on the paint. Rub a mitt across them and each particle becomes a tiny piece of sandpaper dragged across the clear coat. Do that every week for a year and the paint goes from mirror to milky.

The two-bucket method is the simplest way to stop it, and it is how every Gloss Theory wash starts.

## What the two buckets are for

One bucket holds your wash solution: water and a pH-neutral car shampoo. The other holds plain rinse water. Both have a **grit guard** in the bottom: a plastic grid that lets dirt sink below it and stops the mitt picking it back up.

The routine is:

1. Load the mitt from the wash bucket.
2. Wash one panel, in straight lines, with almost no pressure.
3. Rinse the mitt in the rinse bucket, rubbing it against the grit guard.
4. Only then go back to the wash bucket for fresh solution.

The point is that the dirt you lift off the car ends up in the rinse bucket, not in the soapy water you are about to put back on the paint. After a full wash, the rinse bucket is grey and the wash bucket is still clean. That grey water is the scratching you did not do.

## Pre-wash first

Before any mitt touches the car, we cover it in **snow foam** and let it dwell for a few minutes. Foam clings to vertical panels, softens road film and carries a lot of loose grit off with it when it is rinsed away. The less dirt there is on the paint when the mitt arrives, the less there is to drag around.

If you wash at home without a foam cannon, a thorough rinse from the top down does some of the same job. Take your time over the lower doors and the rear of the car, where most of the grit collects.

## Top down, and the lower panels last

Dirt is not spread evenly across a car. The roof and the hood are relatively clean; the sills, the lower doors and the area behind the wheels are filthy. Wash from the top down, and use a separate mitt, or at least a freshly rinsed one, for everything below the door handles.

Wheels and tires get their own brushes and their own bucket. Brake dust is metallic and abrasive, and it has no business on a mitt that will touch your paint.

## Straight lines, not circles

Wash in straight lines that follow the shape of the panel. If you do leave a fine scratch, a straight one is far less visible than the circular swirl you would leave scrubbing in circles, because it only catches the light from one angle.

Use no pressure. Let the shampoo and the mitt do the work. If something will not come off with a gentle pass, it is bonded contamination (tar, sap, iron fallout) and needs a chemical or a clay bar, not more elbow.

## Drying is half the job

A lot of careful washing is undone by drying. A chamois or an old bath towel dragged across a wet car pulls any grit you missed straight across the paint.

We dry with filtered air first, which blows water out of mirrors, badges and seams and leaves most panels nearly dry, then finish with one gentle pass of a thick, plush microfiber towel. At home, a large drying towel laid flat and patted, or pulled slowly across the panel by its corners, is far kinder than wiping.

Our rinse water is deionised, too. Tap water carries dissolved minerals that are left behind as white spots when it evaporates; deionised water has none, so it can dry on the paint without marking it.

## Is it worth the fuss?

If your car is pale and a few years old, you may never notice the difference. If it is dark, new, or has had paint correction or a ceramic coating, washing is where you either keep that finish or slowly lose it. Two buckets, a grit guard and a soft mitt cost very little. Polishing out a year of swirl marks costs a great deal more.
