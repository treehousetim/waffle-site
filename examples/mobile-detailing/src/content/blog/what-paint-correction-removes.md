Paint correction is one of those phrases that sounds like marketing. It is not. It describes something physical: removing a very thin layer of clear coat so that the surface is level again and reflects light cleanly.

Here is what that means, what it can and cannot fix, and why we measure before we touch anything.

## Your paint is mostly clear

A modern car's paint is a stack of layers. Over the metal sits a primer, then a thin colour coat, then the **clear coat**: a transparent, hard layer that gives the gloss and takes all the abuse. On most cars the whole stack is roughly the thickness of a sheet of paper, and the clear coat is a little under half of it.

Almost every defect you can see in good light is in the clear coat:

- **Swirl marks**: fine circular scratches from washing and drying.
- **Haze** or **marring**: a dull, slightly milky look from automatic car washes and dirty towels.
- **Water spot etching**: rings left where mineral-heavy water dried and ate into the surface.
- **Light scratches**: from bags, keys, coats and branches.
- **Holograms**: shimmering trails left by a previous, badly finished machine polish.

## How correction works

A scratch looks like a line because its edges catch the light differently from the surface around it. Correction does not fill the scratch: it lowers the surface around it until the edges are gone.

We do that with a machine polisher, a pad and an abrasive polish. A **compound** cuts quickly and removes deeper defects, but leaves its own fine haze. A **finishing polish** cuts very little and refines the surface to a high gloss. Most cars need one or two stages; a few need three.

Between each step, we wipe the panel with a solvent that removes polishing oils. Oils can fill fine scratches and make a panel look better than it is for a few days, until the first wash. A panel wipe shows the truth, so we know the defect is really gone.

## Why we measure first

Clear coat does not grow back. Every pass of a compound takes a few microns off, and there is only so much to take. That is why every correction visit starts with a **paint depth gauge** on every panel.

The readings tell us how much there is to work with, and they reveal things you cannot see: a panel that has been resprayed, an edge where the paint is thin, a door that was polished hard by someone else years ago. On those, we work gently or not at all.

## What correction cannot fix

- **Scratches through the clear coat**. If your fingernail catches in it, it has probably gone into the colour or below. We can often soften the edges so it is far less visible, but polishing it out would take the clear coat too thin.
- **Stone chips**. They are missing paint, not damaged surface. They need touch-up paint.
- **Failing clear coat**. Once the clear coat is peeling or flaking, the panel needs repainting.
- **Dents**. Those are a job for a paintless dent repair specialist.

We tell you about anything in these categories before we start, not after.

## How long it lasts

Corrected paint stays corrected until something marks it again. That something is almost always washing. A brush car wash will put swirls back within weeks. Careful hand washing, or regular visits, will keep it for years.

That is also why correction and [ceramic coating](/packages/ceramic-coating/) go so well together: the coating is harder than clear coat, makes washing easier, and protects the finish you have just paid to restore.

## Do you need it?

Stand next to your car on a sunny day and look at the reflection of the sun in the hood. If you see a crisp point of light, your paint is in good shape and a [full detail](/packages/full-detail/) will do. If you see a starburst of circular scratches around it, correction will make a bigger difference than anything else you can do to the car.
