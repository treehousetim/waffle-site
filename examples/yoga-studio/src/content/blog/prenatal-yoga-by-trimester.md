Pregnancy is not one state. The body that arrives at fourteen weeks is not the body that arrives at thirty-six, and a good prenatal class changes with you. Here is how I think about each stage, and what we do differently as you go.

One thing first: none of this replaces the advice of your midwife or doctor. If your pregnancy is complicated in any way, check with them before you start, and tell me what they said.

## The first trimester: rest, mostly

We do not take new students in prenatal classes before fourteen weeks. That is not because yoga is dangerous in early pregnancy. It is because the first trimester is often a time of exhaustion and nausea, when the most useful practice is lying down, and because many people are not ready to tell a room full of strangers yet.

If you practised yoga before you were pregnant, you can usually keep coming to gentle classes, telling the teacher, and avoiding anything that makes you hot, breathless or squeezed through the middle. Restorative is a lovely place to be in these weeks.

## The second trimester: room to move

This is when most people join us, and when many feel best. Energy often returns. The belly is growing but not yet in the way.

In class we focus on:

- **the pelvis and hips**, which are loosening under the influence of hormones and need strength around them as much as stretch
- **the upper back and shoulders**, which start to round forward as the breasts and belly grow
- **the breath**, which we begin practising for labour: long, slow exhales, and sounds that help you let go

We leave out deep twists through the belly, lying flat on the back for long periods, and strong core work like crunches. You will notice we never ask you to lie on your front.

## The third trimester: making space

In the last weeks the work changes again. Balance shifts, the ribs get crowded, and sleep can become elusive. We slow down, use the wall and chairs more, widen every stance, and spend more time on all fours, which takes the weight of the baby off your back and is wonderful for an aching lower spine.

We also practise positions for labour: upright, forward-leaning, moving. Partners are welcome to learn them too in the seasonal birth partners workshop.

The final rest is always on your side, propped with a bolster between the knees and a pillow under the head. It is, many people tell me, the best sleep of their week.

## What a class gives you, beyond the poses

At the start of every prenatal class we sit in a circle and say our names, how many weeks we are, and how the week has been. It takes ten minutes, and it is often the part people miss most afterwards. A room of people who understand exactly what you mean by "my ribs" is a rare thing.

Friendships made in that circle tend to carry on long after the babies arrive. Several of our postnatal walking groups began in it.

## When to stop

Whenever you like. Some people come until the week before their due date; some stop at thirty weeks because they would rather be at home. Both are right. Listen to your body, which will be talking to you more clearly than ever.

If you would like to start, the six-week prenatal course keeps your place on a Tuesday or Thursday morning, or you can drop in on the intro offer. Either way, tell me how many weeks you are when you arrive, and we will go from there.
