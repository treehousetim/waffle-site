Book your child’s first dental visit around the time their first tooth appears, and by their first birthday at the latest. It will be short, it will be gentle, and it is mostly about getting to know each other. If you remember one thing from this post, make it this: the goal of a first visit is not a perfect check-up. It is a child who leaves thinking the dentist is a nice place.

## Why so early?

Parents are often surprised that we want to see a one-year-old with four teeth. There are two reasons. The first is practical: baby teeth can get cavities as soon as they come through, and we would much rather spot a chalky white patch early than meet it later as a toothache.

The second reason matters more. A child who comes in every six months or so from the start grows up thinking a check-up is ordinary, like a haircut. A child whose first visit happens because something hurts learns a very different lesson.

## How to talk about it at home

Children read our faces far more than our words. If you are anxious about dentists yourself (plenty of parents are, and that is fine), try to keep it matter-of-fact.

- **Use plain, positive words.** “The dentist is going to count your teeth and see how strong they are.”
- **Skip the reassurances that backfire.** “It won’t hurt” and “be brave” both quietly suggest that it might hurt and that bravery will be needed.
- **Play dentist.** Count your child’s teeth with a spoon handle, then let them count yours, or their teddy’s.
- **Read a picture book** about a visit to the dentist in the week before.

## What happens in the room

For very young children, the whole thing often happens on your lap. You sit facing me, your child lies back with their head in my lap, and we look at the ceiling together. I count teeth out loud, which most toddlers find hilarious, and look at their gums and how the teeth are coming through.

With slightly older children we start with a ride in the chair. Up, down, back, and up again. They can hold the mirror, see the “tooth camera” and feel the little air blower on their hand. Once they know what everything does, there is nothing left to be scared of.

Depending on age, we might also:

- paint on a fluoride varnish, which takes about a minute and tastes faintly of fruit;
- give the teeth a quick polish, if your child is happy to;
- talk with you about brushing, bottles, dummies and snacks.

## If your child is having none of it

Some children cry. Some refuse to open their mouth. Some decide the floor is a better place to be than the chair. All of that is normal, and none of it is a failure.

If a visit is not going well, we stop, and try again another day. Sometimes a child needs two or three short visits to watch, touch and explore before they are ready for a full look. That time is well spent: it means years of easy visits afterwards. Please tell us when you book if your child is anxious, and we will give you a longer, quieter slot.

## After the visit

Make a fuss about how well they did, whatever happened. Your child will leave with a sticker and a new toothbrush; you will leave with a date for next time and, I hope, a few answers.
