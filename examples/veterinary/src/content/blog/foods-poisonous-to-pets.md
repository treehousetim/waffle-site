Most of the poisonings we treat happen in the kitchen, on a lazy weekend, with something the family eats every day. Here are the ones we see most often in Bramblewick, what they do, and what to do if it happens to you.

## The first rule: call, don't wait

If your pet has eaten something on this list, call us straight away, even if they seem completely fine. Many poisons take hours to show signs, and by then the best moment to treat has passed. If we're closed, call the after-hours emergency hospital.

Please don't try to make your pet sick at home. Salt water and other home remedies can be dangerous in themselves, and some poisons do more damage coming back up. We can give a safe medicine that empties the stomach within minutes, if that is the right thing to do.

Bring the packet, the plant, or a photo of it. The exact product and the amount make a real difference to the treatment.

## In the kitchen

**Chocolate.** Contains theobromine, which dogs and cats break down very slowly. Dark chocolate and baking cocoa are far more dangerous than milk chocolate. Signs: vomiting, restlessness, a racing heart, tremors and, in large doses, seizures.

**Grapes, raisins, currants and sultanas.** Can cause sudden kidney failure in dogs. We don't know why some dogs are badly affected and others aren't, and there is no safe amount, so we treat every case. Watch hot cross buns, fruit cake and trail mix.

**Xylitol.** A sweetener in sugar-free gum, mints, some peanut butters, baking mixes and toothpastes. In dogs it causes a sudden, severe drop in blood sugar within half an hour, and can damage the liver. Check the label on any peanut butter you use for treats.

**Onions, garlic, leeks and chives.** Raw, cooked or powdered, they damage red blood cells. Cats are especially sensitive. Gravy, stuffing and baby food often contain them.

**Macadamia nuts.** Cause weakness, wobbliness and fever in dogs. Usually not life-threatening, but miserable.

**Alcohol and raw bread dough.** Dough rises in the warm stomach and ferments into alcohol. Both can be dangerous even in small amounts for small pets.

## In the house and garden

**Lilies.** For cats, every part of a true lily is toxic: petals, leaves, pollen, even the water in the vase. A few licks of pollen groomed off the fur can cause kidney failure. If you have a cat, the simplest rule is no lilies in the house.

**Human medicines.** Ibuprofen, naproxen and other anti-inflammatories cause stomach ulcers and kidney damage in dogs and cats. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) can kill a cat. Keep pill organisers out of reach, and never give your pet a human painkiller.

**Antifreeze.** Tastes sweet and is very poisonous. A lick from a puddle can be fatal without fast treatment. Wipe up drips at once and store it high.

**Slug, snail and rat bait.** Different baits work in completely different ways, and treatment depends on which one it was. Keep the packet.

**Other plants to watch:** sago palms, daffodil and tulip bulbs, autumn crocus, foxglove and oleander. Dogs who dig in the garden are the ones we worry about.

## Making the house safer

- Keep chocolate, raisins and gum in high cupboards, not on the coffee table or in handbags on the floor.
- Close the kitchen door when you're cooking for guests, and ask visitors not to feed the dog.
- Put bin lids on properly. Cooked bones, corn cobs and fatty scraps cause plenty of trouble too.
- Choose cat-safe flowers for the house: roses, sunflowers and orchids are fine.
- Save our number and the after-hours number in your phone today.

## The calm version

Most pets who eat something they shouldn't are absolutely fine, especially when they're seen quickly. If in doubt, call. We'd much rather hear "it was only one raisin" from you on the phone than see your dog three days later. Our [pet resources page](/resources/) has a printable list of dangers to keep on the fridge.
