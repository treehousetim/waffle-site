Spaying and neutering are the operations we do most often, and for most pets they are very routine. But it's still your pet, and it's still surgery, so it's completely normal to be nervous. Here's exactly what happens, from the night before to the stitches coming out.

## Why we recommend it

For cats and rabbits, neutering is a clear win: it prevents unwanted litters, uterine infections and cancer in females, and fighting, spraying and roaming in males. Neutered rabbits are calmer and can live happily in pairs.

For dogs, it's more individual. Spaying prevents a serious womb infection called pyometra and reduces the risk of mammary cancer. Neutering males prevents testicular problems and some prostate disease. For some large and giant breeds, waiting until they're fully grown may be better for their joints. We'll talk through the right timing for your dog at a puppy visit.

## The night before

- **No food after about 10 pm** for dogs and cats. Water is fine overnight.
- **Rabbits and small pets should not fast.** Bring some of their usual food and hay.
- Give a short walk in the morning so your dog has been to the toilet.
- Remove collars with tags that jangle in the kennel, but bring the lead.

## Surgery day

**Drop off, 8 to 8:30 am.** A nurse checks your pet over, confirms your phone number and talks you through the consent form, including the estimate. We may suggest a blood test before anaesthetic, especially for older pets.

**Pre-medication.** Your pet gets a calming injection and pain relief before anything else, so they're relaxed and comfortable.

**Anaesthesia.** A registered nurse monitors your pet the whole time: heart rate, breathing, oxygen, blood pressure and temperature. Patients lie on a warm pad, and most get fluids through a drip.

**Surgery.** A spay removes the ovaries and usually the womb; a neuter removes the testicles. We use dissolvable stitches under the skin wherever we can, so there's often nothing to take out.

**Waking up.** Your pet wakes in a warm, quiet ward with a nurse nearby. **We phone you as soon as they're awake**, and arrange a time to collect them, usually mid to late afternoon.

## Coming home

A nurse goes through everything at collection, and you'll get it in writing too. The headlines:

- **A quiet evening.** Your pet may be sleepy and a little wobbly. Offer a small, light meal and water.
- **Pain relief.** Give the medicine exactly as prescribed, even if your pet seems fine.
- **No licking.** Use the cone or the soft recovery suit. Licking is the most common reason wounds open or get infected.
- **Rest.** Lead walks only for dogs, to the garden and back, for ten to fourteen days. No running, jumping or rough play. Keep cats indoors.
- **Check the wound** twice a day. A little pinkness is normal. Swelling, discharge, bleeding or an open wound are not.

## The recovery fortnight

We'll see your pet for a free check two or three days after surgery, and again at ten to fourteen days, when any skin stitches come out. Most pets feel completely normal well before they're allowed to behave normally, which is the hard part for owners. Puzzle feeders, sniffing games and short training sessions help a lot.

## Call us if

- your pet is very quiet or won't eat the day after surgery,
- the wound is swollen, weeping, bleeding or open,
- your pet is vomiting repeatedly, or seems in pain despite the medicine,
- a rabbit hasn't eaten or passed droppings within 12 hours of coming home.
