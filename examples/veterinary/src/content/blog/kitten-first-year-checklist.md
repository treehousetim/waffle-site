Kittens arrive tiny, fearless and entirely in charge. The first year sets them up for the next fifteen or more, so here is everything we cover with new kitten owners, roughly in the order it comes up.

## In the first week

**Book a first check.** Within a week or two of bringing your kitten home, come in for a once-over. We look at eyes, ears, teeth, heart, belly and coat, check for fleas and ear mites, and weigh them so we have a starting point.

**Set up a safe room.** A quiet room with food, water, a litter tray (well away from the food), a scratching post and somewhere to hide. Let your kitten explore the rest of the house a room at a time.

**Leave the carrier out.** Put it in the safe room with a soft blanket inside, and drop treats in it. A kitten who naps in the carrier at home is a kitten who walks into it calmly on vet day.

## 8 to 16 weeks: vaccines and worming

Kittens need a short vaccine course because the protection they got from their mother fades at an unpredictable time.

- **At 8 to 9 weeks:** the first core vaccine against panleukopenia, cat flu (herpesvirus and calicivirus).
- **At 12 weeks:** the second core vaccine, and rabies where the law requires it.
- **At 16 weeks:** a final dose for kittens at higher risk, which we will talk through.

Feline leukaemia vaccine is worth it for any kitten who will go outdoors or live with cats who do. For a cat who will genuinely live indoors for life, we may skip it after the kitten course.

Worm every two weeks until 12 weeks old, then monthly until six months. Kittens very often have roundworms from their mother's milk, even when they look perfectly well.

## Fleas, and the microchip

Use a flea treatment made for kittens, at the right weight, every month. Never use a dog product on a cat: some dog flea treatments contain permethrin, which is toxic to cats.

We fit a microchip at a vaccine visit. It takes a second, most kittens are too busy with a treat to notice, and we register it for you. Indoor cats escape too, and a chip is how they come home.

## Four to six months: neutering

We recommend neutering at around five months, before a female's first season and before a male starts spraying, roaming and fighting. Neutered cats live longer, safer lives and do not add to the many kittens already looking for homes.

It is a day visit: in first thing with no breakfast, home in the evening, with pain relief and a recheck a few days later.

## Food, water and weight

- Feed a complete **kitten food** until about a year old. It has the extra energy and protein growing bodies need.
- Small meals, often. Many cats like to graze, which suits them.
- Cats are not great drinkers. A water fountain or several bowls around the house helps, and some wet food in the diet is a good idea.
- Pop in for a free nurse weigh-in every month or so. It is the easiest way to catch a kitten who is growing too fast or not fast enough.

## Play, scratching and handling

A kitten who learns that hands are toys becomes a cat who bites hands. Use wand toys and balls, never fingers, and give lots of short play sessions a day.

Scratching is a need, not naughtiness. Offer a tall, sturdy post near where your kitten sleeps, and reward them for using it.

Spend a minute a day gently touching paws, ears and mouth, with a treat each time. You are building a cat who will let you trim claws, give tablets and have their teeth checked, and that is worth more than any toy.

## When to call us sooner

Kittens are small and can go downhill fast. Call us the same day if your kitten:

- is not eating, or is very quiet and sleepy,
- has diarrhoea for more than a day, or any vomiting with lethargy,
- has runny eyes and sneezing with a poor appetite,
- is breathing fast or with an open mouth.
