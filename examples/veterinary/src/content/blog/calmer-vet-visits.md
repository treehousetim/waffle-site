I've been a vet nurse for a long time, and the most common thing owners say to me in the waiting room is "Sorry, he's not normally like this." Of course he isn't. He's somewhere strange that smells of other animals, and last time he came, somebody took his temperature.

The good news: fear of the vet is learned, which means it can be unlearned, or never learned in the first place. Here's what helps.

## Start at home, long before the appointment

**Cats: make friends with the carrier.** For most cats, the scary part of a vet visit starts when the carrier comes out of the cupboard. Leave it out all the time instead, in a room your cat likes, with the door open, a soft blanket inside and the occasional treat. Many cats start napping in it. A top-opening carrier is best: we can examine your cat without tipping them out.

**Dogs: practise handling.** Every day, gently touch your dog's paws, ears, mouth and tail for a second or two, with a treat each time. Lift a lip, hold a paw, look in an ear. You're teaching your dog that being handled predicts good things.

**Both: practise the car.** If the only car trips go to the vet, the car becomes scary too. Short trips to somewhere nice break the link.

## Come for a happy visit

A happy visit is an appointment where nothing happens. You come in, your pet sniffs around the waiting room, steps on the scales, gets a fuss and a few treats from a nurse, and goes home. That's it. They're free, and they make a remarkable difference, especially for puppies and kittens. Ask at the front desk.

## On the day

- **Bring their favourite treats,** and come a little hungry (unless they need to fast for a procedure).
- **Cover the cat carrier** with a towel, and put it on a chair or your lap, not the floor where dogs can sniff it.
- **Wait in the car** if your dog finds other dogs stressful. Give us a ring when you arrive and we'll call you straight into a room.
- **Stay calm and chatty.** Pets read our worry. A relaxed voice helps more than lots of reassurance.
- **Tell us what your pet hates.** Nail trims? Having their back end looked at? We'll plan around it.

## What we do differently

We try to make every visit low-stress, and these are some of the ways:

- separate waiting areas for cats and dogs, and a quiet room for exotics and nervous pets,
- calming pheromone diffusers in the rooms,
- non-slip mats on the table, or no table at all: we're happy to examine on the floor or in your lap,
- warm towels for cats, and as many breaks as it takes,
- treats, lick mats and squeezy pastes to keep busy mouths happy,
- the scary bits last, and only what's needed.

## When fear is severe

Some pets are so frightened that a visit is genuinely distressing for them, and handling them safely is hard for everyone. For these pets, we can prescribe a calming medicine to give at home a couple of hours before the appointment. It doesn't sedate them heavily; it takes the edge off the fear, so the visit goes better, and the next one is easier. Tell us when you book, and we'll talk it through.

## It's worth the effort

A pet who is relaxed at the vet gets better care. We can feel the belly properly, listen to the heart clearly and spot problems sooner. And it's nicer for everyone, including the pet.
