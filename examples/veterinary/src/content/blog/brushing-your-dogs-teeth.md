Every week someone tells me, a little sheepishly, that they have never brushed their dog's teeth. That is fine. Most people haven't, and most dogs have never been asked. The good news is that almost every dog can learn to enjoy it in about two weeks, a minute a day.

## Why bother?

By the age of three, most dogs have some gum disease. Plaque builds up on the teeth, hardens into tartar, and pushes bacteria under the gum line, where it causes inflammation, bone loss, loose teeth and a lot of quiet pain. Dogs rarely stop eating because of a sore mouth; they just get on with it.

Brushing removes plaque before it hardens. Nothing else you can do at home comes close: not chews, not water additives, not special kibble. Those help a little; brushing helps a lot.

## What you need

- **A dog toothpaste.** Never human toothpaste: it foams, it is not meant to be swallowed, and some contain xylitol, which is poisonous to dogs. Dog pastes come in chicken or beef flavour, which helps.
- **A soft brush.** A dog toothbrush, a finger brush, or a soft child's toothbrush for small dogs.
- **Treats,** and a calm moment. After a walk is ideal.

## The two-week plan

Go at your dog's pace. If a step makes them uneasy, go back one for a day or two.

**Days 1 to 3: the paste is a treat.** Let your dog lick a little toothpaste off your finger. That's it. Praise, and stop.

**Days 4 to 6: touch the mouth.** Lift the lip gently at the side and rub a pasty finger along the outside of a few teeth. A second or two, then a treat.

**Days 7 to 9: meet the brush.** Put paste on the brush and let them lick it off. Then brush the outside of the big teeth at the side, the canines and the cheek teeth, for a few seconds.

**Days 10 to 14: build up.** Work round the outside of all the teeth, a few seconds each side, in small circles along the gum line. Aim for about 30 seconds a side. You do not need to brush the inside surfaces; the tongue does a decent job there.

Then keep going, daily if you can. Three times a week is still worth doing; once a month isn't.

## Tips from the exam room

- **Short and happy beats long and thorough.** Stop while your dog is still enjoying it.
- **Hold the head from above,** not by the chin. Most dogs prefer it.
- **Focus on the back teeth.** The upper cheek teeth collect the most tartar.
- **Bad breath that suddenly gets worse** is worth a check, not just more brushing.

## When brushing isn't enough

Brushing prevents plaque. It cannot remove tartar that is already there, or fix disease under the gum. If your dog has brown tartar, red or bleeding gums, a broken tooth, or chews on one side, they need a proper dental cleaning under anaesthetic, with X-rays, first. Then brushing keeps that clean mouth clean.

Signs to book a dental check:

- breath that makes you step back,
- red, puffy or bleeding gums,
- brown or yellow build-up on the teeth,
- dropping food, chewing on one side, or pawing at the face,
- a lump on the gum, or a loose or broken tooth.

## What about cats?

Cats can learn too, with even more patience and smaller steps. Many cats do better with a finger brush. Cats are also prone to painful tooth resorption, which brushing does not prevent, so a yearly look in the mouth matters just as much.
