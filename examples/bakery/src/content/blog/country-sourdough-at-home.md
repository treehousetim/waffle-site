This is the loaf the bakery is built on, cut down from a 60-kilo mix to one loaf, and rewritten for a Dutch oven and a home fridge. It takes about 30 hours from start to finish, and you are busy for perhaps 40 minutes of that.

## The numbers

For one loaf of about 900 grams:

| Ingredient | Grams | Baker’s % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White bread flour | 400 | 80% |
| Whole wheat flour | 75 | 15% |
| Whole rye flour | 25 | 5% |
| Water, 80°F | 375 | 75% |
| Active starter | 100 | 20% |
| Fine sea salt | 10 | 2% |

If this is your first loaf, hold back 25 grams of the water and add it only if the dough feels stiff. Whole grain flours vary, and a wet dough is harder to shape.

## Day one

**Morning: feed your starter.** It needs to be at its peak when you mix, about four to six hours after feeding. See [how we feed ours](/journal/feeding-a-sourdough-starter/).

**Early afternoon: mix.** Stir the flours and 350 grams of the water together until there is no dry flour, cover, and leave for 45 minutes. This rest, the autolyse, lets the flour drink and makes the dough smoother with no work from you.

**Add the starter and salt.** Spread the starter over the dough, sprinkle the salt on top with the last 25 grams of water, and squeeze it all through your fingers until it comes back together, about three minutes. It will feel messy, then suddenly not.

**Fold four times.** Every 30 minutes for the next two hours, wet your hand, lift one side of the dough up until it resists, and fold it over the middle. Turn the bowl and do it four times. By the fourth set the dough should be smooth, a little domed and holding its shape.

**Let it rise.** Leave it covered somewhere warm until it has grown by about half, with bubbles on the sides and a jiggle when you shake the bowl. At 76°F that is roughly two more hours after the last fold. Cooler kitchens take longer; go by the dough, not the clock.

**Shape.** Tip the dough onto an unfloured counter and use a bench scraper to round it into a loose ball. Leave 20 minutes. Then flour the top, flip it, fold the bottom third up, the sides in, and roll it towards you into a tight log or ball. Put it seam side up in a basket or bowl lined with a well-floured tea towel. Rice flour sticks least.

**Into the fridge.** Cover the basket with a bag and put it in the fridge overnight, 12 to 16 hours. This is our walk-in cooler, at home. The cold slows the yeast down and lets the flavor develop.

## Day two

**Heat the oven.** Put a Dutch oven with its lid in the oven and heat to 500°F for 45 minutes. Yes, that long: the pot must be heated through.

**Score it.** Turn the cold dough out onto a piece of parchment, seam side down. With a razor blade or very sharp knife held at a shallow angle, cut one confident slash about half an inch deep along one side. Hesitation makes ragged scores.

**Bake.** Lower it into the pot on the parchment, lid on, and turn the oven down to 475°F. Bake 20 minutes with the lid on, then take the lid off and bake another 20 to 25 minutes, until the crust is deep brown: darker than you think. A pale loaf is an underbaked loaf.

**Cool.** At least an hour on a rack before you cut it. The inside is still cooking, and cutting it early makes it gummy. Listen for the crackling as the crust cools: we call it singing.

## When it goes wrong

- **Flat and spreading:** under-proofed or over-proofed. If it had big irregular holes and a tight crumb near the base, it needed longer in the first rise. If it was sticky and slack and collapsed when scored, it went too long.
- **Pale crust:** the pot was not hot enough, or you took the lid off too soon.
- **Dense and gummy:** the starter was not at its peak, or you cut it warm.

Every loaf teaches you something. Our first hundred were bricks. Or skip the 30 hours: there is one [on the counter](/menu/breads/) most mornings until about eleven.
