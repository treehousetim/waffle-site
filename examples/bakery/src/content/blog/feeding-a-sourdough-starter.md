A sourdough starter is flour and water that has been left long enough for wild yeast and bacteria to move in, and fed often enough that they stay. Ours has been fed every night at nine since 2012. Here is the routine, scaled down from a bucket to a jar, and what to do when yours sulks.

## What you need

- A clean glass jar, about a quart, with a loose lid.
- A digital scale. Cups are too vague for this; a starter cares about ratios.
- Unbleached all-purpose or bread flour, and a little whole wheat or rye.
- Water at room temperature. If your tap water smells of chlorine, leave a jug out overnight first.
- A rubber band, to mark the level after feeding.

## The feed: 1:2:2

Every feed is the same three numbers. Keep 25 grams of the old starter, add 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water, stir, and throw the rest away (or bake with it: see below).

We use 80% white bread flour and 20% whole grain rye in our starter. The rye gives the yeast more to eat and makes the starter more forgiving. At home, a tablespoon of rye or whole wheat in every feed does the same job.

1. Stir the starter down and weigh 25 grams into a clean jar.
2. Add 50 grams of water and stir until it looks like milky soup.
3. Add 50 grams of flour and mix until there are no dry bits. It should be as thick as pancake batter.
4. Scrape down the sides, put the band around the jar at the level of the starter, and put the lid on loosely.

## How to tell it is ready

At about 75°F, a healthy starter doubles in four to six hours after a feed, domes on top, smells of yogurt and green apples, and is full of bubbles when you look at the side of the jar. That moment, at or just after the peak, is when to use it in a loaf.

The float test (a spoonful dropped in water floats) is a decent sign, but not a reliable one. We go by the dome, the smell and the band. If it has risen well past the band and is just starting to sink in the middle, use it now.

## How often to feed it

- **If you bake every day**, as we do: feed it once or twice a day and keep it on the counter.
- **If you bake on weekends**: keep it in the fridge, and take it out on Thursday evening. Feed it Thursday night and Friday morning, and it will be lively by Friday evening for a Saturday loaf.
- **If you forget about it for a month**: it is probably fine. Pour off any grey liquid on top (that is alcohol, called hooch, and it means it is hungry), keep a spoonful from the bottom and give it two or three feeds a day until it doubles again.

## When it sulks

**It is not rising.** Usually it is too cold. Find it a warmer spot: the top of the fridge, inside the oven with only the light on, next to (not on) a radiator. Our bakehouse runs at 78°F for this reason.

**It rises and falls in two hours.** It is too warm, or too hungry. Feed it at a stiffer ratio (1:3:3), or use cooler water.

**It smells of nail polish remover.** It is very hungry. Two feeds a day for a couple of days will sort it out.

**There is pink or orange on it.** That is mold, not hooch. Throw it away and start again; it is not worth the risk. Grey liquid is fine; color is not.

## What to do with the discard

Every feed makes 100 grams you do not need. We put ours into the [morning bun dough and the pancakes](/menu/pastries/) for the staff breakfast. At home, keep the discard in a jar in the fridge for up to a week and use it in crackers, waffles, banana bread or pancakes: anything that is raised with baking soda or powder, where the starter adds flavor rather than lift.
