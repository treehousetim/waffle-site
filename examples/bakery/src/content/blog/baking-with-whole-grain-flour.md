A third of all the flour we use is whole grain: wheat, rye and spelt, stone-ground with the bran and germ left in. It makes our bread taste of something. It also makes it behave differently, and if you try it at home without adjusting, you get bricks. Here is what changes, and how to get it right.

## What whole grain flour is

A wheat berry has three parts. The endosperm is the starchy center that becomes white flour. The bran is the outer skin, full of fiber. The germ is the seed’s embryo, full of oils, vitamins and most of the flavor. White flour sifts out the bran and germ; whole grain flour keeps everything.

Stone-ground whole grain flour, milled slowly between stones, keeps the germ’s oils intact. That gives it a sweet, nutty flavor, but it also means it goes stale. Fresh whole grain flour smells sweet; old flour smells of cardboard or paint. Buy it in small bags, check the milling date, and keep it in the fridge or freezer.

## What it changes

- **It drinks more water.** Bran soaks up water slowly. A dough with a lot of whole grain needs more water than a white dough, and a longer rest before you judge it.
- **It ferments faster.** Whole grains carry more of the yeast’s and bacteria’s food, so the dough rises and sours faster. Expect your bulk rise to be shorter.
- **It rises less.** The sharp edges of the bran cut through the gluten network, so whole grain loaves are naturally denser and smaller. That is not a fault.
- **It tastes better.** Which is the point.

## How to swap it in

Start with the [country loaf recipe](/journal/country-sourdough-at-home/), which is already 20% whole grain. Then:

1. **Go up by 10% at a time.** Swap 50 grams of the white flour for whole wheat, bake it, and see how it behaves. Then go further.
2. **Add water as you go.** For every 10% of whole wheat you add, add about 2 to 3% more water. For rye, more.
3. **Rest it longer.** Let the flour and water sit together for an hour, not 45 minutes, before you add the starter and salt. The bran needs the time.
4. **Watch the dough, not the clock.** Check it 30 minutes earlier than the recipe says during the first rise.

## The three grains we use

**Whole wheat.** The easiest to start with. Nutty, a little sweet, and with enough gluten to still make an open loaf at 30 to 40%. Our country loaf and the spelt tin both use it.

**Rye.** Rye has very little gluten that forms a network, so a rye dough is more like a thick paste than a dough. It does not stretch; it is shaped with wet hands and baked in a tin. It loves sourdough, which stops it from going gummy. Our [seeded rye](/menu/breads/) is 40% rye and keeps for a week.

**Spelt.** An older relative of wheat. Its gluten is weaker and more fragile, so a spelt dough wants gentle handling and less mixing. It tastes sweet and a little like honey, which is why we pair it with honey in the spelt tin.

## Where our flour comes from

Most of ours comes from a small mill in the state that grinds grain from farms within a hundred miles. We visit twice a year, and the miller tells us what the harvest was like: a wet summer means softer wheat, and we change the water in the dough to match. You cannot get that from a sack in a warehouse.

If you want to try it, we sell bags of the same whole wheat and rye flour at the counter, a kilo at a time, freshly milled. Ask, and bring a jar for some [starter](/journal/feeding-a-sourdough-starter/) while you are at it.
