The question we hear most at the counter, after "is that still warm?", is how to keep a loaf fresh. The short answer: on a board, cut side down, for three days; then in the freezer. Here is the long answer, and how to bring a tired loaf back.

## Why bread goes stale

Stale bread is not dry bread. As a loaf cools and sits, the starches inside it slowly recrystallize and squeeze out water, which moves to the crust. The crumb gets firm and crumbly, and the crust goes leathery. It is called retrogradation, and it happens fastest at fridge temperature.

That is why **the fridge is the worst place for bread.** A loaf in the fridge goes stale about three times faster than one on the counter. The only exception is in a very hot, humid summer, when mold is the bigger risk.

## The first three days

A long-fermented sourdough keeps better than a yeasted loaf, because the acidity slows staling and mold. Ours keep well for three days, and the rye keeps for a week.

- **Day one:** leave it whole until you need it. A whole loaf stays fresh longer than a cut one.
- **Once cut:** stand it cut side down on a wooden board. The crust protects the rest, and the cut face seals itself against the board.
- **Overnight:** a paper bag, a linen bread bag or a clean tea towel. They let the crust breathe so it stays crisp.
- **Not plastic, at first.** A plastic bag keeps the crumb soft but turns the crust soft and chewy. If you prefer soft crust, a plastic bag is fine from day two.

## Freezing

From day three, freeze it. Bread freezes beautifully, because the freezer stops staling almost completely.

1. Slice it first, so you can take out what you need.
2. Wrap it tightly in a freezer bag with as much air squeezed out as you can.
3. Toast slices straight from frozen. There is no need to thaw.

A whole frozen loaf thaws in about three hours on the counter, still wrapped; then refresh it in the oven as below.

## Bringing a loaf back

A loaf that has gone firm can be made almost new, because heat reverses staling for a while.

- **Run the whole loaf under the tap** for a second or two, so the crust is wet but not soaked.
- **Bake it at 400°F for 8 to 12 minutes**, directly on the rack, until the crust is crisp again.
- **Eat it within a few hours.** A revived loaf stales again quickly once it cools.

For slices, a toaster does the same job, and a hot dry skillet does it better.

## Stale is not wasted

When a loaf really is past it, it is an ingredient.

- **Breadcrumbs:** blitz it, toast the crumbs in a dry pan with olive oil and garlic, and keep them in a jar for pasta.
- **Croutons and panzanella:** torn, oiled and baked, then tossed with tomatoes in summer.
- **Bread pudding and French toast:** better with stale bread, because it soaks up the custard without falling apart.
- **Ribollita:** the Tuscan bean soup that is thickened with old bread. The best use of a stale rye we know.

At the bakery, the loaves left at three o'clock go to the food shelf on Mill Street, and yesterday’s croissants become [almond croissants](/menu/pastries/). Nothing goes in the bin.

## How much to buy

Buy what you will eat in two or three days, and freeze the rest of a big loaf when you get home. If you are on your own, ask for a half loaf: we will cut any [country loaf](/menu/breads/) in two for you.
