Knob-and-tube was the standard way to wire a house from the 1880s to the 1930s. Many Springfield homes still have some of it.

## How to recognise it

In the attic or basement, look for single wires running separately, held off the framing by white ceramic knobs and passing through joists in ceramic tubes. Upstairs, two-prong outlets are a clue, though not proof.

## Why it is a concern now

- **No ground.** Modern appliances and surge protection rely on one.
- **Brittle insulation.** After a century, the rubber and cloth can crumble at a touch.
- **It needs air.** It was designed to shed heat into open cavities. Blown-in insulation around it can make it overheat.
- **Decades of additions.** Most systems have been extended and spliced by people who are no longer around to ask.

## Your options

Leaving it in place is sometimes acceptable if it is in good condition and not buried in insulation, but many insurers will not cover a house with active knob-and-tube. Replacing it does not mean gutting the house: we [rewire through existing cavities](/services/rewiring/) with small openings.

If you are buying an older house, a [pre-purchase inspection](/services/inspections/) will tell you how much remains.
